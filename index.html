Stroke Risk Prediction Model

Research Question

  • Can demographic and clinical variables predict who is at risk of stroke at the population level?

What I Did and How

  • Cleaned and pre-processed a simulated health dataset of 5,000 records using Python (pandas), fixing typos, inconsistent labels, and missing BMI values through mean and median imputation
  • Built and compared two logistic regression models using scikit-learn — train-test split and K-fold cross-validation — to test which method produced more stable results with imbalanced health data
  • Applied Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) to rank 11 variables and selected the 6 strongest predictors: age, BMI, average glucose level, work type, hypertension, and smoking status

Key Findings

  • Stroke occurrence was highest in the 60 to 80 age group, followed by 40 to 60
  • People who had never smoked had more strokes than current or former smokers — challenging common assumptions
  • Private sector workers had the highest stroke occurrence, followed by the self-employed
  • Cross-validation outperformed train-test split with 62.5% accuracy on imputed data versus 50% — showing its advantage when data has missing values
  • Strong positive correlation found between age and marriage status

Public Health Relevance

  • Stroke is the third leading cause of death in Canada and a major driver of long-term disability, costing the health system over $3.6 billion annually
  • Identifying which population subgroups carry the highest risk supports targeted prevention programs and more efficient use of health resources
  • Findings suggest that stroke prevention messaging should extend beyond traditionally recognized high-risk groups to include working-age adults in high-demand employment sectors

STBBI Trend Analysis Among Canadian Youth

Research Question

  • What are the trends and socio-demographic patterns of sexually transmitted and blood-borne infections — Chlamydia, Gonorrhea, Syphilis, and HIV — among Canadians aged 15 to 34 from 2015 to 2023, and how did COVID-19 affect these trends?

What I Did and How

  • Extracted national STBBI surveillance data from PHAC and Statistics Canada, covering rate per 100,000 for all four infections from 2015 to 2023
  • Cleaned, aggregated, and transformed the dataset in Excel, linking infection rates to sex, age group, education level, family income, and unmet healthcare needs
  • Built an interactive Power BI dashboard with dedicated views for trend overview, sex, education, family income, healthcare access, and COVID-19 impact, using Pearson's correlation to quantify associations
  • Calculated percentage change from a 2015 baseline for each disease to track long-term trends and isolate the impact of COVID-19 on reported rates

Key Findings

  • The onset of COVID-19 triggered a decrease in the rate of all four STBBIs except Syphilis, which continued to rise through the pandemic
  • Males reported more cases of Gonorrhea, Syphilis, and HIV than females, while females reported disproportionately higher rates of Chlamydia
  • Tertiary education was protective against Chlamydia and HIV but associated with higher rates of Gonorrhea and Syphilis — education alone did not uniformly reduce risk
  • Syphilis recorded the most alarming growth of the four infections, rising 419% from 2015 to 2022 — more than five times its baseline rate

Public Health Relevance

  • Teenagers and young adults face the highest risk due to elevated sexual activity and risk-taking behaviours, with low socioeconomic status further increasing vulnerability
  • Untreated STBBIs carry broader costs for Canada, including reduced productivity, rising healthcare burden, and long-term population health impacts
  • Findings highlight where socio-demographic targeting — by income, education, and healthcare access — could strengthen provincial sexual health interventions

Missed Appointments Analysis

Research Question

  • What factors contribute to patients missing their medical appointments, and can trends be identified to help reduce future no-shows?

What I Did and How

  • Analysed over 110,000 medical appointment records from Brazil, covering variables such as age, gender, neighbourhood, chronic conditions, wait time, and SMS reminders
  • Pre-processed the dataset in a spreadsheet to remove datetime formatting noise before importing into MySQL
  • Used MySQL to clean, query, and aggregate the data — applying DISTINCT, CASE statements, and aggregate functions to uncover no-show patterns across demographics, conditions, and scheduling factors
  • Calculated no-show rates by age group, gender, neighbourhood, chronic condition, scholarship status, SMS reminder, and day of the week

Key Findings

  • Overall no-show rate was approximately 20%, with an average wait time of 15 days for no-shows compared to 8 days for show-ups — longer waits drive higher no-show rates
  • Younger patients (teens and young adults aged 10–29) had the highest no-show rates at 25%, while seniors (60+) had the lowest at 15–16%
  • Patients who received SMS reminders had a higher no-show rate (28%) than those who did not (17%), likely reflecting selection bias in who receives reminders
  • Patients with chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes had lower no-show rates than the general population, suggesting stronger healthcare engagement
  • One neighbourhood (Ilhas Oceânicas de Trindade) recorded a 100% no-show rate, though it represented only 0.002% of total patients

Public Health Relevance

  • Missed appointments waste healthcare resources and delay patient care — understanding who misses and why is the first step toward smarter scheduling and follow-up strategies
  • The finding that longer wait times increase no-shows has direct implications for appointment booking systems and capacity planning
  • Identifying high-risk neighbourhoods and age groups allows health facilities to target outreach and reminder systems more effectively
